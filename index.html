Executive investment verdict

Void Models is not viable as a model-first company, but it is viable as a software-first, verifier-centric vertical AI company that keeps small-model training as a gated option. Training specialized weights is cheap. What is expensive and slow is selling them, and they lose value within months.

Five facts decide it

  1. The cost case has collapsed. Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna list at $0.10 input / $0.50 output per 1M tokens. A year earlier, Haiku 4.5 listed at $1 / $5. Self-hosted small models now win on price only at high sustained volume, on-premises, or under tight latency.
  2. Weight-level leads expire. Open coding models of 32B or less reached the frontier's SWE-bench score about 7 months after the frontier did (section 11).
  3. Compute is not the constraint. An 8B LoRA SFT run costs ₹650 to ₹1,700, and a GRPO run ₹5,000 to ₹1 lakh. Data, evaluation and sales are the real costs.
  4. Small specialists win on narrow, checkable, stable tasks, mostly on cost-adjusted terms. A strict accuracy win over today's best frontier system with equal tools is uncommon (subjective 15% to 25%) and least likely in coding.
  5. Buyers pay for outcomes, not models. Standalone fine-tuning platforms were absorbed by larger companies (OpenPipe into CoreWeave, Predibase into Rubrik).

How elite models actually become capable

Capability is mostly bought with data quality and compute, then elicited by post-training; architecture and parameter count set the ceiling but rarely explain the gap between two models of the same size.

That single fact drives most of this report: a startup cannot win the pretraining race, but it can sometimes win the elicitation race on one narrow distribution.

The mechanics, in one pass

  • Tokenization. Text becomes integer IDs via byte-pair encoding. This is not neutral: the same sentence can cost up to about 15x more tokens in some languages than English (Petrov et al. 2023, A), which matters for Indian-language products because cost and context scale with tokens.
  • Residual stream. Each token becomes a vector x; every layer adds to it rather than replacing it. Attention moves information between positions; the MLP transforms information at one position (Elhage et al. 2021, B).
  • Attention. Routes information: "which earlier tokens matter for this one". Induction heads, a two-layer attention circuit that copies patterns seen earlier in context, appear abruptly in training and track the onset of in-context learning (Olsson et al. 2022, B).
  • MLP layers. Behave like key-value memories over patterns (Geva et al. 2021, A); factual associations can be located and edited mostly in mid-layer MLPs (Meng et al. 2022, A).
  • Normalization (RMSNorm/LayerNorm) keeps activations in range so deep stacks train stably; it is an enabler, not a capability source.
  • Superposition. Models store far more features than they have dimensions by overlapping them (Elhage et al. 2022, B). This is why capacity is shared: training hard on one domain competes for the same directions other skills use, the mechanical root of forgetting.
Each layer l adds to the residual stream h:
  a(l)     = Attn_l( Norm(h(l)) )
  h(l+1)   = h(l) + a(l) + MLP_l( Norm(h(l) + a(l)) )

Training loss (next-token prediction):
  L = - sum over t of  log p_theta( x_t | x_<t )

Why next-token prediction produces skills

To predict the next token of a Python file well, a model must track scope, types, imports and what a function is meant to do; minimizing loss on enough code forces internal models of syntax and semantics. The same pressure on worked solutions yields arithmetic and algorithm-following, though less reliably than on text that is pattern-rich.

  • What parameters store. Controlled experiments estimate roughly 2 bits of factual knowledge per parameter, independent of size, when facts are seen enough times (Allen-Zhu and Li 2024, B). A 7B model therefore holds on the order of 14 Gbit of facts. That is ample for one domain's procedures, small for "all of medicine plus all of law".
  • Why specialization is possible. A general model spends capacity on everything; a narrow task uses a small subset. Fine-tuning reallocates attention to that subset and fixes output format, but it cannot cheaply add broad reasoning the base never learned.
  • Why it is limited. Fine-tuning on facts the base does not already know is learned slowly and increases hallucination (Gekhman et al. 2024, A). For new knowledge, retrieval beats fine-tuning (Ovadia et al. 2023, B).

Telling generalization from memorization and contamination

Benchmark scores overstate ability whenever test items leaked into training data. Fresh GSM8K-style problems (GSM1k) caused drops of up to about 13 points for some model families (Zhang et al. 2024, A). On SWE-bench Verified, models identified the buggy file path from the issue text alone far more often than on unseen repositories, a memorization signal (Liang et al. 2025, B).

Practical tests you should run on any model you train or compare:

  1. Time split. Evaluate only on items created after every model's training cutoff, as LiveCodeBench does (Jain et al. 2024).
  2. Perturbation. Rename identifiers, reorder clauses, change constants; a memorizer degrades sharply, a generalizer does not.
  3. Private holdout. Keep a test set no model or teacher has ever seen, never sent to an API used for data generation.
  4. Overlap audit. n-gram and embedding overlap between training data and tests; drop near-duplicates.
  5. Out-of-distribution slice. A second customer, framework version or repository the model never trained on.

What has strong evidence, and what does not

Strong evidence

  • More and better data
  • Compute
  • RL with verifiable rewards on checkable tasks
  • Distillation from a stronger model
  • Repeated sampling plus a verifier (Brown et al. 2024)
  • Retrieval for knowledge

Weak or contested

  • "Emergent abilities are sudden phase changes": much of the sharpness is an artifact of all-or-nothing metrics (Schaeffer et al. 2023).
  • "Chain-of-thought shows the model's true reasoning": explanations can be unfaithful (Turpin et al. 2023).
  • "RL teaches fundamentally new skills": contested. At large k, base models often solve as many problems as RL-tuned ones (Yue et al. 2025); prolonged RL can expand the solvable set (Liu et al. 2025, ProRL); and random rewards improved one model family on math, so some "RL gains" are elicitation of pre-existing behaviour (Shao et al. 2025).
  • "A long context window means the model uses it": accuracy drops for information in the middle of long inputs (Liu et al. 2023).

How elite coding models are actually built

Elite coding ability now comes from reinforcement learning in executable environments on top of very large, code-heavy pretraining, not from clever fine-tuning of small models.

A small model can beat a frontier model only on narrow, verifiable, high-volume micro-tasks, and even then the usual win is equal accuracy at a fraction of the cost and latency, not higher accuracy.

The pipeline, separating verified facts from speculation

  1. Collect code legally. Permissively licensed GitHub code with opt-out, deduplicated: The Stack v2 covers 619 languages and was used for StarCoder2 (Lozhkov et al. 2024, B). Licensing risk is real: copyleft and unlicensed code, secrets and PII must be filtered; litigation over training on public code is ongoing in several jurisdictions.
  2. Filter and deduplicate aggressively. OpenCoder's fully open ablations showed file-level dedup and quality filtering matter more than raw volume (Huang et al. 2024, B). phi-1 showed curated "textbook" code beats 100x more raw code at small scale (Gunasekar et al. 2023).
  3. Pretrain or continue pretraining at enormous scale. Qwen2.5-Coder used about 5.5T code-related tokens (Hui et al. 2024); DeepSeek-Coder-V2 continued from a general checkpoint with 6T more tokens (DeepSeek-AI 2024). Repository-level packing (files ordered by dependency) and fill-in-the-middle objectives teach cross-file reasoning and editing.
  4. Synthetic instruction data. OSS-Instruct seeds problems from real code snippets and measurably improves small models (Wei et al. 2023, B). Synthetic data helps most when it is executed and filtered; unverified synthetic code mostly teaches the teacher's mistakes.
  5. Execution feedback and RL. Rewards from unit tests and compilers: early CodeRL (Le et al. 2022), then large-scale RLVR. DeepCoder-14B reached 60.6% on LiveCodeBench, near o3-mini-low, trained with RL on about 24k verified problems using 32 H100s for 2.5 weeks (Agentica/Together 2025, B).
  6. Agentic, repository-level RL. Models learn to navigate repos, run tests and edit across files in sandboxes. Open recipes: SWE-Gym (Pan et al. 2024), SWE-smith's 50k synthesized task instances (Yang et al. 2025), R2E-Gym (Jain et al. 2025), Meta's SWE-RL (Wei et al. 2025). Qwen3-Coder's team described running about 20,000 parallel environments for agent RL (Qwen blog, July 2025, C).
  7. What frontier labs do beyond this (S). Claude, GPT and Gemini pipelines are not public. Their reports and system cards confirm RL on real-world coding tasks in many environments; scale of environments, reward models and human data is speculation.

A telling industry data point. Cursor's Composer 2 (March 2026) was built on an open-weight base, Kimi K2.5 (about 1T parameters), with continued pretraining and a large RL scale-up; Cursor said roughly three-quarters of total compute was its own (DataCamp summary, C). Even a richly funded coding company specializes a very large open base rather than pretraining or using a 7B model.

Why benchmark wins do not equal developer productivity

In a randomized trial, 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with early-2025 AI tools while believing they were about 20% faster (METR 2025, A). METR's 2026 follow-up was judged unreliable because developers increasingly refused to work without AI, biasing results; METR believes speedups are now larger but cannot size them (METR, 2026, B).

Reasons benchmarks mislead:

  • Tasks are pre-specified with tests; real work is under-specified.
  • Benchmarks reward a single patch; real work rewards maintainability and review cost.
  • Scaffolding and repo context dominate outcomes.

What makes a comparison fair

Section 8 operationalizes this:

  • Same repository context and retrieval
  • Same tools (compiler, test runner) for both sides
  • Same attempt budget (pass@1 versus pass@1, or equal cost)
  • A contamination-free test set built after every model's cutoff
  • Current frontier models at reasonable effort settings, not last year's or a crippled prompt

Beating GPT-4o, a zero-shot prompt, or a model denied tools proves nothing about today's frontier.

What is unlikely to work

Training a small model to be "the best Django model" or "the best React model". Frameworks are well covered in frontier pretraining, tasks are open-ended, and frontier vendors improve on exactly these every quarter.

Training-method comparison: what works, when, and why

For a startup on a single rented GPU, the winning stack is data work plus cheap weight updates: verifier-filtered distillation into a LoRA adapter, optionally followed by small-scale RL with a verifiable reward, wrapped in retrieval and tools.

Pretraining from scratch and continued pretraining are the methods most likely to burn money without beating a newer open base model.

The memory and compute arithmetic

Training memory has five parts: weights, gradients, optimizer states, activations, and temporary buffers. For P parameters with AdamW in mixed precision:

Full FT memory = 2P (bf16 weights) + 2P (bf16 grads) + 4P (fp32 master) + 8P (Adam m, v)
               = 16P bytes, divided by N GPUs with ZeRO-3 / FSDP

LoRA memory    = 2P + 16 x P_LoRA + activations
QLoRA memory   = about 0.52P + 16 x P_LoRA + activations
P_LoRA         = r x (d_in + d_out) per adapted matrix

Activations per layer without recomputation are about s x b x h x 34 bytes once FlashAttention removes the quadratic attention term (Korthikanti et al. 2022). For an 8B-class model (h = 4096, 36 layers) at 4,096 tokens and batch 1, that is about 20 GB; with activation checkpointing it drops to about 1.2 GB of saved layer inputs plus one layer recomputed, at the price of about 33% more compute.

C_full = 6ND   (8ND with activation checkpointing)
C_LoRA = 4ND   (6ND with activation checkpointing)
t      = C / (peak FLOP/s x MFU)

N = parameters, D = training tokens

LoRA skips weight gradients for frozen weights but still back-propagates through them, so it saves roughly a third of compute, not 99%.

Worked example (D): 90M training tokens of LoRA SFT on an 8B model is about 4.3 x 10^18 FLOPs, about 3.5 hours on one H100 at an assumed 35% MFU, or about 21 hours on one RTX 4090. The GPU bill for a typical SFT run is single-digit dollars; iteration, data and evaluation are where money goes.

Method cards

Open any method for how it works, what it changes, what it costs and how it fails.

Pretraining from scratch builds knowledge; you cannot afford to win

Minimizes next-token loss over trillions of tokens; updates all weights. It builds knowledge and latent skills and nothing else does that. A 1B model on 1T tokens needs about 6 x 10^21 FLOPs, roughly 4,200 H100-hours (about $11k to $17k, ₹10 to 16 lakh) at 40% MFU, and would still trail open models of the same size trained on about 36T tokens (Qwen3 report).

Failure mode: you build a worse copy of something free. Suitability: none.

Continued / domain-adaptive pretraining (DAPT) only for genuinely new vocabulary

Same loss on a domain corpus; updates all weights (or high-rank adapters). Helps when the domain has vocabulary and patterns absent from general data, e.g. biomedical text or a proprietary language (Gururangan et al. 2020).

Risks: forgetting general and instruction-following ability unless you replay general data and re-do instruction tuning; mitigations include learning-rate re-warming and replay (Ibrahim et al. 2024). Needs 100M+ clean domain tokens to matter.

Cost: an 8B model on 1B tokens is about 6.4 x 10^19 FLOPs, about 45 H100-hours, or $120 to $180. Evidence A for domain-text tasks; weak for "make it smarter".

Full-parameter SFT 16 bytes per parameter, multi-GPU

Cross-entropy on responses only; updates all weights. Needs 16 bytes/param plus activations, so an 8B model needs about 2 to 4 x 80 GB GPUs with FSDP. Gains over LoRA appear mainly with large datasets (Thinking Machines 2025, B). Forgets more than LoRA (Biderman et al. 2024, A).

LoRA the default adapter; trains 0.1% to 2% of parameters

Freezes W and learns a low-rank update ΔW = BA with rank r; only A and B (typically 0.1% to 2% of parameters) are trained, not every weight.

Matches full fine-tuning on small-to-medium SFT datasets and in RL even at very low rank, but underperforms on large datasets; apply it to all layers including MLPs, and use about 10x the full-FT learning rate (Thinking Machines 2025, B; Hu et al. 2021, A). LoRA "learns less and forgets less" than full FT on code and math (Biderman et al. 2024). Fits one 24 GB GPU at 8B in bf16 with checkpointing.

QLoRA 4-bit base, ideal on 16 to 24 GB consumer cards

Base weights quantized to 4-bit NF4 and frozen; LoRA adapters trained in bf16 through the dequantized weights. Enabled a 65B fine-tune on one 48 GB GPU (Dettmers et al. 2023, A). Slightly slower per step than LoRA and a small quality cost in some settings; ideal on 16 to 24 GB consumer cards.

Other PEFT: DoRA, IA3, prefix and prompt tuning, GaLore none changes the picture

DoRA decomposes magnitude and direction and sometimes edges LoRA (Liu et al. 2024); prompt tuning is much weaker at small scale; GaLore projects gradients to cut optimizer memory for full-rank training (Zhao et al. 2024). None changes the strategic picture; LoRA is the default.

Knowledge distillation (logit-level) needs a teacher you run yourself

Student minimizes KL divergence to the teacher's token distribution (Hinton et al. 2015). Richer signal than hard labels, but requires the teacher's logits, so the teacher must be an open model you run. On-policy variants train on the student's own samples scored by the teacher, fixing exposure bias and improving sample efficiency (Agarwal et al. 2024, GKD, B).

Sequence-level distillation / teacher-generated data powerful, but check the teacher's terms

Train on teacher outputs as targets (Kim and Rush 2016). DeepSeek-R1's distilled 14B and 32B Qwen models beat o1-mini on AIME 2024 from about 800k teacher samples (DeepSeek-AI 2025, A).

Legal constraint: Anthropic's terms forbid using outputs to train models that compete with Anthropic's, listing "using Outputs as training targets" as an example (Anthropic, March 2026); OpenAI's terms have a similar no-competing-models clause. A model you sell as a coding or general assistant is plausibly "competing". Use permissively licensed open teachers (check each model's license file) or the customer's own data.

Rejection sampling and best-of-N fine-tuning most cost-effective when a verifier exists

Sample N candidates, keep those a verifier accepts, fine-tune on them; iterate (STaR, Zelikman et al. 2022; RFT, Yuan et al. 2023). This is the single most cost-effective method when a verifier exists, because the verifier converts cheap samples into clean data.

DPO and relatives: IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO tone and preferences, not correctness

Optimize a closed-form objective so chosen responses become more likely than rejected ones relative to a reference model (Rafailov et al. 2023). Good for tone, preference and refusal behaviour; weak for correctness unless pairs come from a verifier. Prone to length exploitation and over-optimization.

RL with verifiable rewards: PPO and GRPO cost dominated by sampling

PPO uses a learned value function (Schulman et al. 2017); GRPO drops it, normalizing each sample's reward against a group of samples for the same prompt (Shao et al. 2024), with fixes in DAPO (Yu et al. 2025) and Dr. GRPO (Liu et al. 2025).

Group-normalized advantage for sample i of G:
  A_i = (r_i - mean(r_1..r_G)) / std(r_1..r_G)

Objective, with probability ratio rho_i:
  J = E[ (1/G) x sum_i min( rho_i x A_i, clip(rho_i, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) x A_i ) ]
      - beta x KL( pi_theta || pi_ref )

Worked example on Tinker (D): 300 steps x 64 prompts x 8 samples x 3k tokens = about 460M sampled tokens; on Qwen3-8B at $0.60 sample + $0.44 train per 1M that is about $480 (₹46k); on Qwen3.5-9B about $1,040 (Tinker pricing, 19 Oct 2026).

Failure modes: reward hacking (tests that pass trivially), entropy collapse, gains that are elicitation rather than new skill (Yue et al. 2025), and spurious-reward effects on some model families (Shao et al. 2025). RL forgets less than SFT at matched gains (Shenfeld et al. 2025, B).

Curriculum learning and multi-stage training SFT for format, RL for reliability

Order data easy to hard, or run SFT then RL. Multi-stage (SFT for format, RL for reliability) is standard and well-supported; fine-grained curricula show inconsistent gains. Cheap to try once the pipeline exists.

Data-mixture optimization mix 10% to 30% general data

Choose domain weights to minimize loss on a target (DoReMi, Xie et al. 2023; RegMix, Liu et al. 2024). In fine-tuning, the practical version is: always mix 10% to 30% general instruction data to limit forgetting, then tune the ratio against your eval.

Self-training / iterative improvement only with a strong external verifier

Model generates, verifier filters, model retrains, repeat. Works with a strong external verifier; without one, models amplify their own errors and diversity collapses.

Model merging zero compute, unpredictable results

Average or combine fine-tuned weight deltas (task arithmetic, Ilharco et al. 2022; TIES, Yadav et al. 2023; DARE, Yu et al. 2023). Zero compute, useful for combining a domain adapter with general skills; results are unpredictable and must be re-evaluated every time.

Mixture-of-experts a base-model choice, not a training project

Routes each token to a few expert MLPs so total parameters grow without per-token compute (Fedus et al. 2021; Mixtral). For you this is a base-model choice, not a training project: MoE bases like Qwen3.6-35B-A3B serve fast because only about 3B parameters are active, but fine-tuning them needs memory for all experts.

Retrieval-augmented generation beats fine-tuning for adding facts

Injects knowledge at inference (Lewis et al. 2020); beats fine-tuning for adding facts (Ovadia et al. 2023). Updates no weights; cost is engineering plus a vector or keyword index.

Tool-augmented models the scaffold can move results by tens of points

The model calls compilers, databases, search, calculators (Toolformer, ReAct). In coding, the scaffold can move results by tens of points (see the frontier table above), more than most fine-tunes.

Test-time scaling and search coverage becomes accuracy only with a verifier

Spend more inference compute per query: repeated sampling raised SWE-bench Lite coverage from 15.9% with one sample to 56% with 250 (Brown et al. 2024, A); compute-optimal test-time scaling can let a smaller model match a roughly 14x larger one on problems of moderate difficulty (Snell et al. 2024, B). The catch: coverage gains only become accuracy if a verifier can pick the right sample.

Small model plus verifier, and hybrid systems where small models earn their keep

A small model proposes; tests, compilers, schema validators or a judge accept or reject; failures escalate to a frontier model. This is where small models earn their keep, and it is how you separate weight gains from system gains (section 12). NVIDIA argues many agent sub-calls are repetitive enough for small models (Belcak et al. 2025, position paper, C).

How methods combine

Strong combinations

  • RAG + SFT (format) + verifier
  • Rejection-sampled SFT, then GRPO on the same verifier
  • LoRA adapters merged or hot-swapped per customer

Weak combinations

  • DAPT then little instruction data (breaks chat ability)
  • DPO on unverified pairs
  • RL without a held-out eval (reward hacking goes unseen)

Specialized-model performance limits

On a narrow task, post-training mostly converts what a base model can sometimes do into what it reliably does; it rarely adds what the base cannot do at all.

The single most predictive measurement is the base model's pass@k on your task with a verifier: if the base never solves an item in 64 tries, cheap training will almost never make it solve that item on the first try.

Does capacity limit a specialist?
Demonstrated

Knowledge storage is bounded (about 2 bits/param, Allen-Zhu and Li 2024); small models trail badly on long-horizon agentic repair (section 3).

Plausible

A narrow procedure (schema extraction, one compiler's errors) needs a small fraction of a 7B model's capacity.

Unsupported

A 3B to 14B model matching frontier breadth inside a whole profession.

Can training recover missing abilities?
Demonstrated

Format, schema adherence, domain style, and transferred reasoning traces from a stronger teacher (DeepSeek-R1 distills).

Plausible

Domain procedures the base saw rarely but did see.

Unsupported

Injecting large new factual knowledge by SFT; it raises hallucination (Gekhman et al. 2024).

Can LoRA approach full fine-tuning?
Demonstrated

Yes for RL and small-to-medium SFT; gap on large datasets (Thinking Machines 2025).

Plausible

Gap is small below about 100M training tokens.

Unsupported

LoRA equals full FT for continued pretraining on billions of tokens (Biderman et al. 2024 shows it does not).

When is continued pretraining necessary?
Demonstrated

New vocabulary or script, proprietary languages, domain corpora with 100M+ tokens (Gururangan et al. 2020).

Plausible

Low-resource Indian languages; a customer's internal DSL.

Unsupported

For a well-covered framework such as Django or React.

Does RL create new strategies?
Demonstrated

It reliably raises pass@1 and response length on checkable tasks.

Plausible

Long RL can expand the solvable set (ProRL).

Unsupported

That a few hundred GRPO steps on a 7B model teach abilities absent from its pass@64; at large k, base models often match RL models (Yue et al. 2025).

Can verifiers and tools compensate for capacity?
Demonstrated

Yes: repeated sampling plus a checker multiplies solved problems (Brown et al. 2024); scaffolds move coding scores by up to about 30 points (Liu et al. 2026).

Plausible

A 14B model plus strong verifier matching a frontier model on a checkable task at lower cost.

Unsupported

Tools rescuing a model on tasks with no checkable output.

Does inference-time search change the ceiling?
Demonstrated

A smaller model with compute-optimal test-time search can beat a roughly 14x larger one on easy and medium problems, not on the hardest (Snell et al. 2024).

Plausible

Best-of-8 with a verifier closes most of the gap on routine tasks.

Unsupported

Search fixing hard reasoning failures.

Forgetting and negative transfer
Demonstrated

Fine-tuning erodes general skills (Luo et al. 2023); LoRA and RL forget less (Biderman et al. 2024, Shenfeld et al. 2025).

Plausible

Mixing 10% to 30% general data limits damage.

Unsupported

Forgetting being irrelevant because "we only need one task": customers send off-distribution inputs.

Diversity versus specialization
Demonstrated

Narrowly tuned models learn shortcuts: a LoRA-tuned 3B classifier was the most keyword-dependent system tested, while the frontier zero-shot model was the least (Huynh and Pham 2026).

Plausible

Diverse paraphrased training data reduces shortcutting.

Unsupported

That high in-distribution accuracy predicts production accuracy.

Diminishing returns
Demonstrated

SFT accuracy grows roughly log-linearly in data size on reasoning tasks (Yuan et al. 2023).

Plausible

Each doubling of clean data buys a roughly constant number of points until a plateau.

Unsupported

Linear returns to more data or compute.

Where specialization clearly works, with numbers

  • A fine-tuned Qwen3.5-27B scored 95.0% on a 51-subcategory security-document taxonomy versus 75.4% to 79.9% for unnamed commercial models under the same prompt, trained on 78,358 examples (arXiv 2605.20368, May 2026, C because comparators and prompts are not disclosed). The pattern: a fixed, fine-grained label scheme the frontier model was never told in enough detail.
  • 310 LoRA fine-tunes across 31 tasks beat their bases by 34 points and GPT-4 by 10 points on average (Zhao et al. 2024, LoRA Land, B). These were classic NLP tasks against a 2023-era model; frontier zero-shot today would close much of that gap.
  • Counter-evidence that must be weighed equally: GPT-4 with prompting alone beat the specialized Med-PaLM 2 on MedQA, 90.2% versus 86.5% (Nori et al. 2023, B), and GPT-4 outperformed the domain-pretrained BloombergGPT on most financial tasks (Li et al. 2023, B).

The rule that reconciles these: specialization wins when the target is a narrow, stable, labelable decision space with abundant in-distribution examples; generalists win when the task needs broad knowledge, open-ended judgment, or adapts faster than you can collect labels.

A pipeline that approaches the useful ceiling

  1. Freeze three test sets first: in-distribution test, private holdout never sent to any API, and an out-of-distribution slice (new customer, new library version).
  2. Measure baselines: base model prompted; current frontier prompted with the same context; your no-training system (retrieval + tools + verifier).
  3. Measure the base's pass@k curve for k = 1, 4, 16, 64. The gap between pass@1 and pass@64 is your realistic headroom for training.
  4. SFT via LoRA on verifier-filtered data distilled from a permissively licensed teacher, at 1k, 2k, 4k, 8k, 16k examples, to see the data-scaling slope.
  5. Rejection-sampling rounds on the student's own outputs (on-policy data), one to three rounds.
  6. GRPO with the same verifier only if pass@16 is well above pass@1 after SFT.
  7. Test-time layer: best-of-N with the verifier, then escalation to a frontier model on failure or low confidence.
  8. Regression checks on general skills and the OOD slice after every stage.

Evidence that more training is no longer worth paying for

Stop investing in weights when any two of these hold:

  • The last doubling of clean training data added less than 1 point on the private holdout, inside the confidence interval.
  • pass@1 is within 2 to 3 points of the base's pass@16: RL headroom is used up.
  • Dev-set gains no longer show up on the OOD slice.
  • A newly released open base of the same size matches your fine-tune zero-shot or with a prompt.
  • The cost per point exceeds the value per point.

Serving: when does self-hosting pay?

The cost comparison that matters is not "my 8B versus Opus"; it is "my 8B versus the cheapest acceptable hosted model". Cheap frontier-lab models now list at $0.10 input / $0.50 output per 1M (Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6 Luna), and hosted 8B-class open models at $0.06 to $0.25.

Break-even tokens per month = (GPU $ per hour x 730) / hosted $ per token (blended)

A dedicated L4 at $0.44/hr costs about $321 a month. Against Haiku 5.5 at a blended $0.20 per 1M (3:1 input:output), break-even is about 1.6B tokens a month, an average of about 620 tokens per second, around the clock. Against Sonnet 5.5 at a blended $4 per 1M it is only 80M tokens a month.

Conclusion: self-hosting a small model beats mid-tier frontier pricing easily, but beats the cheapest frontier tier only at sustained high utilization. Privacy, on-premises requirements and latency, not price, are the stronger reasons to self-host. For fine-tuned models with bursty traffic, serverless multi-LoRA hosting at base-model price avoids idle GPUs (Fireworks).

Data: how much, from where

  • CI repair training: 5,000 to 20,000 verified repair pairs. Sources: (a) public commits on MIT/Apache/BSD repositories where CI went red then green with a small diff; (b) error injection into compiling code (rename a symbol, change a type, drop an import, bump a library version), keeping the original as the target, verified by compile and tests; (c) repairs sampled from a permissively licensed open teacher and kept only if they compile and pass tests. Record licenses per file; exclude copyleft and unlicensed code.
  • Document training: 1,000 to 5,000 labeled pages. From pilot customers with written consent for training, plus synthetic documents rendered from real templates; teacher labels from an open model are kept only when validators pass and a human spot-check agrees.
  • Legal guardrails: no outputs from Claude, GPT or Gemini as training targets (see training methods); frontier models are evaluated, never distilled. Note the consequence: a teacher weaker than the frontier cannot by itself produce a student that beats the frontier, so any strict win must come from verification, RL against the checker, or proprietary customer data.

Metrics

pass@k = mean over tasks of [ 1 - C(n - c, k) / C(n, k) ]
n = 5 samples per task, c = samples that pass, C = binomial coefficient
  • Task success: compiles, all original tests pass, a hidden test set added by you passes, no test was deleted or weakened, no new any, # type: ignore or @ts-ignore (these are reward hacks).
  • Severity-weighted failure rate (SWFR): silent wrong fix that compiles but fails hidden tests = 10; reward hack = 5; still fails to compile = 1; explicit abstain or escalate = 0.5. SWFR = sum of weights / number of tasks.
  • Cost per successful task: (inference + verifier compute + escalation cost + training cost amortized over 12 months of projected volume) / successes.
  • Latency: p50 and p95 wall-clock including compile loops.
  • Reliability: run-to-run standard deviation over 5 seeds; calibration of confidence (does a high-confidence answer pass more often?); abstention precision.

Reliability outside the benchmark

What the ranking turns on

  • Willingness to pay beats technical elegance. Indian SMB software price points (₹2k to ₹50k a year for compliance tools) leave almost no room for support and acquisition costs (see unit economics). Mid-market and foreign buyers pay 5x to 20x more for the same hours saved.
  • Checkable outputs are the only place where small models reliably win (see coding models and performance limits). Document reconciliation, CI repair and revenue-cycle coding all have checks; tutoring, legal advice and open-ended support mostly do not.
  • Retrieval and ordinary software solve most of the problem cheaply. In documents, a schema, validators and reconciliation rules do more than a fine-tune; in support, a good knowledge base does more than a model.
  • Compliance is a moat only once you can afford it. India's DPDP Rules were notified on 13 November 2025 with most data-fiduciary duties effective from 13 May 2027 (Mondaq summary); US healthcare work needs HIPAA business-associate agreements. Data-residency and on-premises demands from these rules are where a private small model becomes a selling point.
  • Defensibility against cheaper frontier models is strongest where you hold workflow integration and proprietary labeled data (healthcare admin, a vertical document workflow) and weakest where the frontier vendor ships the same feature (coding assistants, enterprise search).

Domains to avoid as a first business

Absent a specific distribution advantage, avoid as a first business: generic coding copilots, enterprise knowledge assistants, education apps, Indian-language consumer chat, and legal drafting for Indian SMBs. Each has at least one serious problem for a bootstrapped founder: free frontier substitutes, platform bundling, or buyers who do not pay. These are broad markets, not verdicts on every workflow inside them; privileged access to a sharply defined workflow (for example through a family trade network) can reverse the ranking.

How to get the first paying customer without building a model

  1. Pick one workflow and one buyer type you can reach in two weeks: CA firms through your network, a software agency, or US SMBs via cold outreach.
  2. Run 15 discovery calls. Quantify hours per month spent, error cost, and what they pay today. Collect sample inputs with permission.
  3. Deliver a concierge pilot in week 3: frontier API + rules + validators + you as the human in the loop. Charge for it (₹10k to ₹25k, or $500 to $1,500); free pilots teach you nothing about willingness to pay.
  4. Build the eval set from their real data (with consent and a DPA). This, not the model, is the first proprietary asset.
  5. Convert to a monthly contract priced on outcome or volume.
  6. Train weights only when volume, latency or an on-premises requirement makes the serving break-even favourable, and the experiment thresholds say training beats the system.

Failure analysis and defensibility

The strongest case against a model-centric Void Models is that weight-level advantages expire within months: open coding models of 32B or less have reached the frontier's SWE-bench score roughly seven months after the frontier did, Anthropic cut its cheapest tier's price 10x in a year, and the best-known fine-tuning platforms were absorbed into larger companies. What compounds is data rights, evaluation assets, workflow integration and distribution.

Qwen3.6-27B reached 77.2% in April 2026, the score Claude Sonnet 4.5 posted in September 2025. Any fine-tune lead you build on today's base faces the same clock.

Recommended technical architecture

Build a verifier-centred system in which the model is a swappable part: structured context, a cheap worker model, deterministic tools and checks, retries, and escalation to a frontier model. Train your own LoRA adapter only when the logs show it beats the cheapest acceptable hosted model on cost per verified success.

The runtime path runs top to bottom in the centre; anything the verifier rejects is retried, then escalated, then sent to a human; every verdict feeds the eval and training sets on the left, and a new adapter replaces the worker only if it passes the eval gates.

Separating gains from better weights versus better system design

Run a 2x2 on the same tasks: base or tuned weights, each with a bare prompt and with the full system. With accuracies B0 (base, bare), BS (base, system), T0 (tuned, bare), TS (tuned, system):

weights effect = 1/2 x [ (TS - BS) + (T0 - B0) ]
system effect  = 1/2 x [ (BS - B0) + (TS - T0) ]
interaction    = TS - BS - T0 + B0

A negative interaction is the common case: tuning helps less once the system is good. Then add one more arm, the best frontier model inside your system. If it beats your tuned model by more than your cost saving is worth, the system is the asset and the weights are optional.

    Fail rule: fail a gate, fix it once; fail again, pivot to a software-first company or stop.

    Gate 2 is the Void Models decision: a Pareto win under the experiment's rules keeps model training in the company; anything less turns Void Models into a software-first company that may still use small models as a cost lever.

    Precise definitions for the gates

    • Accuracy bar (Gate 1): agreed with the customer before the pilot, measured on their data, for example 97% field-level accuracy on extracted documents or 70% merged-without-edit CI fixes.
    • Pareto win (Gate 2): non-inferior to the best frontier system within the margin the eval can resolve (4 points on a 1,000-task eval), at no more than one third of its full cost per accepted result and no more than the cheap tier's (see Experiment design). Where the customer requires on-premises data, Gate 2 is instead met by hitting their accuracy bar at acceptable cost.
    • Gross margin (Gate 3): revenue minus inference, hosting, payment fees and support labour, excluding founder salary.
    • Ported in under a week (Gate 4): a new open base model fine-tuned and evaluated on frozen test sets within 7 days; customer acceptance and migration may take longer, but the pipeline, not one checkpoint, is the asset.

    Investment committee decision

    Decision: decline Void Models as a model-first company; back a software-first, verifier-centric vertical AI business in small tranches, with small-model training as an option exercised only at Gate 2.

    The model-first path has a low chance of revenue inside a year and its main asset, fine-tuned weights, depreciates within months; the software-first path reaches revenue in weeks and accumulates the data and evals that make training worthwhile later.

    Answers to the five questions

    Short answers to the five questions the research set out to settle.

    Q1. Can a 3B to 14B specialized model genuinely outperform today's strongest frontier models on a narrow, valuable task? Under exactly what conditions?

    Yes, but rarely on raw accuracy against the best current system given equal tools; usually on cost-adjusted terms. A strict accuracy win needs all of:

    1. a narrow, stable input distribution;
    2. machine-checkable outputs;
    3. thousands of verified in-distribution examples the frontier never saw, such as non-public document formats, a proprietary label scheme or a customer's own history;
    4. a frontier competitor given the same context, tools and attempt budget;
    5. a gain that holds on fresh, private and out-of-distribution tests with clustered statistics.

    Subjective odds: 15% to 25% overall, under 5% in coding, about 35% on non-public local formats. Open-ended software engineering is not such a task: the best open models of 30B or less trail the best closed systems by roughly 10 to 18 points on SWE-bench Verified.

    Q2. What is the highest-leverage affordable method to achieve this without renting massive GPU clusters?

    A deterministic verifier and a frozen eval set first; then rejection-sampled distillation from a permissively licensed open teacher into a LoRA adapter on a 9B to 27B open base; served with best-of-N against the verifier and escalation to a frontier model; GRPO only if pass@16 sits well above pass@1 after SFT. Compute per cycle is ₹1,000 to ₹10,000 on one rented GPU or a managed API. Because a permitted open teacher is weaker than the frontier, the decisive signal must come from the verifier and from customer data, not from the teacher.

    Q3. Is training our own model weights necessary to build a profitable business, or an avoidable expense?

    Avoidable at the start. Train only when at least one holds: inference is 15% to 25%+ of revenue and a small model cuts full cost per accepted result by half at equal verified accuracy; the customer requires on-premises, air-gapped or data-resident processing; a latency budget cheap APIs cannot meet; or a proprietary label space where prompting plateaus below the agreed accuracy bar. Even then it means a LoRA adapter on an open base, never your own pretraining.

    Q4. What is the most realistic way to reach the first ₹1 lakh in revenue, and then a sustainable company?

    Paid concierge pilots in one vertical with checkable outputs: for example four pilots at ₹25,000 with mid-size Indian CA practices, logistics firms or customs brokers drowning in messy local documents, or one or two projects at $1,000 to $2,500 for overseas small businesses with the same workflow. Deliver with cheap and frontier APIs, validators, and yourself in the loop. Then convert pilots to monthly contracts priced on volume or outcome (at least ₹4,000 a month in India, $49 to $500 abroad), productize the repeated workflow, keep contractual data rights, and move through Gates 2 to 4; adapters come only when Gate 2 passes.

    Q5. If the evidence does not support the hypothesis, what company would I build with these skills and this capital?

    The same company without the model-lab ambition: a verifier-first document and reconciliation automation business for accounting workflows, starting with Indian CA firms and mid-market finance teams, then, once SOC 2 or ISO 27001 is affordable, India's offshore accounting centres serving US firms. The US accountant workforce was about 1.78 million in 2024, roughly 10% below 2019, and firms such as RSM US, Moss Adams, Sikich and CohnReznick are expanding in India (Allwork, citing Reuters, C).

    Expect competition from Thomson Reuters, Wolters Kluwer, BlackLine and funded AI accounting startups, so win on one painful local workflow first. Second choice: an evaluation-and-reliability service that builds eval suites, regression tests and verifiers for companies deploying LLMs, which uses exactly the skills this plan develops.
